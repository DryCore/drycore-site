What we are building

Closed loop. Three targets. Measured, or not claimed.

DryCore is developing a closed-loop heat-rejection system with three design targets: zero water consumption, lower energy per watt of heat rejected in high ambient temperatures, and low enough noise to ease siting.

HT
Heat, captured
The building-side circuit stays closed. Evaporation is not the rejection step.
RJ
Heat, rejected
The mechanism under test is meant to avoid the usual trade: save water, then spend the savings on fans and compressors at design-day ambient.
AC
Sound, bounded
Noise is specified as a siting variable, not a later mitigation project.
BN
Bench, instrumented
Pass and fail thresholds are published before the first run. That is the method. It is also the discipline.

What this page will not do

No performance figures appear on this site, because none have been measured yet. That is what the bench work is for. A number invented for a meeting would be the opposite of the standard the company is using.

Validation sequence

  • BeforeThresholds written down: water use, energy per watt rejected at high ambient, acoustic signature.
  • DuringInstrumented bench cell. The run does not get to renegotiate the threshold.
  • AfterPass, revise, or stop. Operator conversations are about fit, including why an approach like this might not fit a given site.

Where it sits in the plant

Rejection is the unsolved edge.

Not a chip cooler

Cold plates and immersion are upstream. DryCore is aimed at the step after the heat has left the rack: getting it out of the building.

Not a cooling tower

Towers are efficient because they evaporate. The design target is the water number those towers cannot post: zero consumption at the rejection step.

Not a louder dry cooler

Conventional dry rejection trades water for fan power and, often, for noise. Both of those are treated here as first-order constraints.

Operators

We want the objection.

We are speaking with data center operators, developers, and thermal engineers about what actually limits heat rejection at their sites — including why an approach like this might not fit.
