The problem

Heat has to leave. The usual ways out are closing.

An AI data center has to move an enormous amount of heat out of the building, continuously. The common method evaporates water to do it. The alternative, a sealed dry loop, solves the water problem and pays for it in fan and compressor energy on exactly the hottest afternoons.

Water

Evaporation is consumption.

Two different quantities get quoted interchangeably, so this page keeps them apart. Withdrawal is what a facility takes in: a Pacific Institute review in 2026 reported individual sites withdrawing roughly 270 to 3.9 million gallons per day. Consumption is what never comes back. EPRI models one 150 MW hyperscale facility consuming about 340,000 gallons a day at a water-use efficiency of 0.36 L/kWh, and about 2.7 million gallons a day on a water-cooled chiller at 2.8 L/kWh — roughly eight times the water for the same compute, decided by nothing but the cooling architecture. Those are modelled scenarios under stated assumptions, not measured plants. Most of what a cooling tower withdraws evaporates and does not return.

Grid

The penalty arrives at the worst hour.

Dry coolers and mechanical refrigeration avoid the water bill. On a design-day afternoon they spend fan and compressor energy precisely when wholesale power is scarcest and the interconnection is already the binding constraint on the campus.

Siting

The third veto.

Communities now treat water draw and noise as grounds to delay or block a site. A thermal design that ignores the fence line is incomplete, even if the PUE model looks fine in a spreadsheet.

What operators already know

Liquid inside the rack does not finish the job.

Direct-to-chip and immersion move heat off the silicon more cleanly. They do not, by themselves, reject it to ambient. Somewhere at the building line that heat still has to cross into air, and that crossing is where water, power, and noise are decided.

Hyperscalers are already publishing zero-water and low-water designs. Microsoft has described fleet water-use effectiveness falling from 2.3 liters per kWh in early generations to 0.27 in 2025, with most owned sites on low- or zero-water cooling. The direction of travel is not in dispute. The open question is a rejection method that stays efficient when the air outside is hot, and quiet enough to permit.
